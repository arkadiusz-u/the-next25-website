From furnace to plug: how heavy industry is going electric

Steel, cement and chemicals built the modern world. The next chapter is about making them without the fire, and the plug is what replaces it.

By The Next 25Feature · 5 min readThe electricity era · 2026
Scroll to read

Almost everything around you was made in heat. The steel in the bridge, the cement under the street, the glass in your window: all of it forged at temperatures most of us will never stand near. For two centuries that heat has come from burning fossil fuels, and it is why industry still produces roughly a quarter of the world's CO2. These are the hardest fires on earth to put out.

The urgency is not abstract. 2024 was the warmest year on record, and the first in which global temperatures likely held above 1.5°C for an entire year. Energy-related emissions hit an all-time high of 37.8 gigatonnes. The power sector is decarbonizing fast; industry now has to catch up.

The last fire

Here is the part that surprises people: this is no longer a technology problem. Heat pumps and electric boilers already handle industrial processes up to 500°C, which covers food, textiles, pulp and paper, and much of chemicals today, with high-temperature applications advancing quickly. As the paper behind this story puts it, industrial electrification is no longer constrained by the absence of technology, but by the pace and scale of deployment.

"The technology exists. What's missing is the speed, and the grid to plug into.Decarbonizing Industry · The Next 25

What's actually in the way

Three things, mostly. Industrial equipment lives for decades, so every replacement cycle missed locks in another generation of emissions. Electricity is often taxed against itself: in the EU, taxes and levies average 26% of the final electricity price for industry, and reach 55% in some markets, while the fossil alternative goes untaxed. And the connections factories need can be the slowest part of all: large grid projects still take up to 13 years from plan to power.

None of these barriers is technical. They are choices about planning, pricing and permitting, which is precisely why they can be unmade.

Five moves, one decade

The paper closes with five recommendations: plan grids for 2050 rather than for next year; build and modernize them ahead of demand; fix the taxes and rules that still favor fossil fuels; deploy the proven technologies at speed and scale; and close the skills gap in the workforce that has to do all of it. None of them is glamorous. All of them are buildable.

And the economics have quietly flipped. In the United States, every dollar invested in recommended transmission expansion returns between $4.30 and $5.80, with payback in under three years. A cleaner industrial future is not a cost to be endured. It is the best infrastructure investment of the decade.
