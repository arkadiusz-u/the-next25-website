The transition has a waiting list

The world has decided to electrify. It has not yet built the factories that make the equipment to do it.

By The Next 25Feature · 5 min readThe electricity era · 2026
Scroll to read

Every wind farm, every data centre, every electric railway and every heat pump has something in common that never makes the launch photo: a transformer, a switchgear bay, a length of cable, a converter. Somebody has to make them. Right now, not enough somebodies can.

This is the least visible bottleneck in the energy transition and, the paper argues, one of the most decisive. You can approve a grid and finance a grid. You cannot install a grid you cannot buy.

The size of the order

The International Energy Agency's numbers are the ones to remember. By 2040 the world must add 65 million kilometres of transmission and distribution lines and replace a further 45 million. That is the length of every grid on earth today, built again, in about fifteen years.

Behind it sits a queue. By 2026 more than 2,500 gigawatts of generation, storage and large-load projects were waiting for a grid connection worldwide. Much of that wait is equipment.

"Greenfield projects can take up to five years to complete, and brownfield expansions up to three.Strengthening grid technology supply chains · The Next 25

Why you cannot just build more factories tomorrow

A new plant for large power transformers takes up to five years from decision to first delivery. Expanding an existing one takes up to three. The specialist steel, the copper, the skilled winders and testers all have their own lead times. Add trade disputes and the occasional surge in demand and you have a supply chain that cannot flex at the speed the transition is asking it to.

The paper's recommendations are about agility: long-term commitments from customers so manufacturers can invest ahead of orders, more standardised designs, and manufacturing closer to the markets it serves.

Building the factories first

Hitachi Energy has made the bet the paper describes. A $9 billion global programme to expand capacity and people, the industry's largest. A historic $1 billion in American manufacturing. $300 million in China, $195 million in Canada, $150 million in Latin America, new transformer capacity in India, Türkiye, Sweden, Spain and Finland.

None of it is a product. All of it is the reason the products arrive. The transition has a waiting list. The job is to make it shorter.
