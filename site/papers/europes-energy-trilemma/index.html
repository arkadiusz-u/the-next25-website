Cheap, clean, secure: pick three

Energy policy has always been a trade-off between security, sustainability and affordability. The grid is the one investment that stops them pulling against each other.

By The Next 25Feature · 5 min readThe electricity era · 2026
Scroll to read

For as long as there has been energy policy there has been a triangle. Make power cheaper and you often make it dirtier. Make it cleaner and you may make it less reliable. Make it more secure and someone pays. Europe has lived inside that triangle for decades, and the last few years of shocks made every corner sharper.

The report, written with Aurora Energy Research, makes an unfashionable argument: the way out of the triangle is not a better generator. It is a better grid.

Electrification has barely started

Here is the surprise. Between 2015 and 2021 renewables grew from 29 to 38 per cent of Europe's electricity. Over the same period, fossil fuels fell from 73 to just 70 per cent of the overall energy mix. Electricity's share of final demand moved from 22 per cent in 2010 to 23.5 per cent in 2024. The clean electricity is arriving. The economy is not yet running on it.

Meanwhile the system is straining. The volume of power that had to be managed around congestion reached 72 terawatt-hours in 2024, up from 57 in 2021. Demand grew far faster than grid investment over the last decade.

"A 25% increase in cross-border interconnection capacity enables around 27 TWh of additional solar and wind power utilisation and reduces gas-fired generation by ~35 TWh in 2040.Europe's Energy Trilemma Rewired · The Next 25

What the modelling found

Aurora ran the numbers. Increase Europe's cross-border interconnection by a quarter, and in 2040 the continent uses around 27 terawatt-hours more wind and solar that would otherwise be wasted, and burns about 35 terawatt-hours less gas, roughly eight per cent less. Cheaper, cleaner and more secure, from one investment.

The investment is real money. Grid spending needs to reach around €120 billion a year to 2050, against roughly €74 billion today, while EU electricity demand could rise 40 per cent by 2035. Set against the cost of the alternative, it is the better deal.

Wiring the continent

Interconnection is a physical thing: converter stations, subsea cables, transformers, and the control systems that let two national grids behave as one. It is the work Hitachi Energy has done since it pioneered HVDC in 1954.

Today that means Elmed, the first direct-current link between Europe and North Africa, the offshore connections that bring North Sea wind ashore for TenneT, and the multi-terminal system at Shetland. Each one is a side of the triangle getting shorter.
