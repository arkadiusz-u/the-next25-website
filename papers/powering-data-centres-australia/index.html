The data centre arrives in three years. The power line takes ten.

Australia has become one of the most attractive places in the world to build a data centre. Its grid is discovering what that means.

By The Next 25Feature · 5 min readThe electricity era · 2026
Scroll to read

Imagine ordering a house that will be delivered in three years, on a street that will not exist for ten. That, in one sentence, is the problem the report describes.

Australia ticks every box a data-centre developer wants: land, abundant renewable energy, political stability, strong data governance and proximity to the region's big markets. Investment is arriving. And some of the facilities being proposed are larger than any single customer the local network has ever connected.

A new kind of customer

Data centres used about 3.9 terawatt-hours in Australia's National Electricity Market in 2024–25. By 2049–50 the report projects around 34 terawatt-hours, close to ten per cent of all underlying demand. That is a new category of load arriving in a system that is already retiring coal, absorbing record amounts of solar and wind, and building more transmission than at any point in its history.

Most of it wants to sit in the same crowded urban corridors, close to the people and the fibre, where the network is already full.

"This mismatch between load growth and infrastructure delivery has emerged as the principal constraint on data centre integration.Powering Data Centres: Australia · The Next 25

Why the rules are changing

Australia has not waited. Federal, market and state frameworks now expect data centres to help pay for new renewable capacity, to support network investment, to be flexible with their demand where they can, and to meet efficiency standards. The idea is simple: if you are going to be the biggest customer on the street, you help build the street.

The report says three things will decide how fast it works. Connection processes that give developers and planners transparency and speed instead of a queue. Enough new capacity, utility-scale and on-site, to meet the targets. And flexibility, so that a facility can shift some of its load when the system is tight.

Closing the ten-year gap

Some of the answer is building more lines. More of it is getting more out of the lines that exist, grid-enhancing technologies, equipment that keeps a renewable-heavy system stable, and software that runs the whole thing intelligently.

Hitachi Energy is already part of that work in Australia, strengthening Queensland's grid for Powerlink, supporting Victoria's largest grid-scale battery with AusNet, and delivering the Darwin–Katherine battery system in the Northern Territory. The house is coming. The street has to be ready.
