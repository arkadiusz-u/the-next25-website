Europe decided wind is a security policy. Now it needs the wires.

After the energy shocks of the last few years, Europe stopped treating renewables as a climate measure and started treating them as protection. That changed what the grid has to do.

By The Next 25Feature · 5 min readThe electricity era · 2026
Scroll to read

There is a quiet change in how Europe talks about wind and solar. A few years ago they were the climate answer. Now they are also the energy-security answer: power that no one can switch off from outside, at a price no one can hold to ransom.

That decision is popular and it is working. But it comes with a condition nobody puts on a poster. All that new, cheap, home-grown electricity has to be connected, carried and balanced, and the grid that does it was built for a very different century.

How fast the mix is turning over

The International Energy Agency expects wind and solar to make up around 46 per cent of the EU's generating capacity by 2030, up from 30 per cent in 2025. Great Britain has set itself a harder sum: to reach its target of 95 per cent low-carbon power by 2030, wind and solar need to supply 77 to 82 per cent of generation, from 36 per cent today.

At the same time, the old thermal plants that used to steady the system are retiring, and millions of small sources, rooftop panels, batteries, electric cars, are joining it from the edges. The grid is no longer a few big power stations feeding predictable demand. It is a vast, fast, two-way system.

"Security of supply is no longer just about adequate generation capacity; grids must maintain reliability at every moment.Grids in Transition: Europe · The Next 25

Why the bill shows up in your electricity price

The European Commission's own Electrification Action Plan names the culprits behind high electricity prices, and none of them is a shortage of clean generation. They are insufficient grid planning, congestion, too little storage and too little flexibility. In other words: the power is there, the wire is not. Britain's system operator reached the same conclusion and warned that delaying network reinforcement simply moves the cost onto consumers later.

The report describes five pressures arriving together: demand that is rising and harder to predict, variable renewables, retiring thermal plants, distributed resources, and growing cyber, climate and physical threats. They push on five parts of the system at once, its capacity, stability, reliability, flexibility and resilience.

What a grid built for this looks like

It plans across borders rather than inside them. It uses technology to carry more on lines that already exist and to keep a system full of inverters stable. It secures the equipment years ahead. And it pays for the wire the way it pays for the wind farm, as infrastructure, not an afterthought.

This is Hitachi Energy's home ground. The company is delivering the offshore links for TenneT with Larsen & Toubro, the first multi-terminal HVDC system in Europe at Shetland, and the first-ever HVDC link between Europe and North Africa with Elmed. Europe made the decision. The wires are how it keeps it.
