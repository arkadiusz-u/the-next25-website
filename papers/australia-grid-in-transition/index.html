Australia has the sun. It is waiting for the wire.

The clean power is being built. The prices are set to fall. Both depend on transmission arriving on time, and that is the part running late.

By The Next 25Feature · 5 min readThe electricity era · 2026
Scroll to read

There is a version of Australia's energy story that sounds finished. Record rooftop solar, big batteries, a renewable target for 2030 and net zero by 2050. The generation side of the sum is going well. The other side of the sum is a wire.

Electricity demand is expected to almost double, from 205 terawatt-hours in 2025 to 389 terawatt-hours by 2050, as cars, homes and industry switch to electricity. The clean power to meet it is mostly in places far from the cities, which means it has to travel.

The delivery problem

Australia is building more transmission than at any time in the history of its National Electricity Market, and it is running behind. Some critical projects have slipped by up to five years. All the new lines are now expected to be complete by 2042–43.

The country's competition watchdog has warned what that means in plain terms: delays to transmission raise costs for consumers, because cheap power that cannot reach you is not cheap.

"Transmission delivery has emerged as one of the most significant challenges to the transition.Australia: Electricity Grid in Transition · The Next 25

The prize on the other side

Get the wire built and the reward is immediate. The report notes that electricity prices in the market could fall by around five per cent by 2030 as low-cost renewables come online. That is the affordability argument of The Next 25 in a single country: paying for the grid is not the expensive choice.

It is also the security argument. A system that depends on wind in one state and sun in another is only secure if the two are connected, and if the equipment in between can keep it stable when the weather changes.

Building the link

Hitachi Energy's part in Australia is specific. It is delivering Powerlink's Central Queensland synchronous condenser project to strengthen the core of the state's system, and the Marinus Link that will let Tasmania's wind and hydro storage flow in both directions to Victoria. It has supported Victoria's largest grid-scale battery connection with AusNet and installed a long-term service agreement with Alinta Energy.

None of it generates a single watt. All of it decides whether the watts arrive.
