The country that suddenly needs a lot more electricity

For ten years America's appetite for power barely moved. Then it jumped, and the grid that was built for a quieter decade is being asked to carry a louder one.

By The Next 25Feature · 5 min readThe electricity era · 2026
Scroll to read

Think of the electricity grid as a road network you never notice until it jams. For most of the last decade, American traffic was steady. Then, in the space of two years, everyone decided to drive at once.

Between 2023 and 2025, electricity sales in the United States grew by 4.7 per cent. That is more growth than the seven years before it added together. The government's own forecasters now expect another 14 per cent by 2035. Most of the new traffic comes from one kind of building: the data centre.

Where the new demand comes from

In 2024, data centres used about 192 terawatt-hours of electricity, nearly five per cent of everything the country consumed. Depending on how fast artificial intelligence spreads, researchers at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory expect that to reach somewhere between 521 and 843 terawatt-hours by 2030. Add factories coming home from overseas and cars, homes and heating switching to electricity, and the picture is clear.

The catch is where it lands. The growth is concentrated in a few places, above all Virginia and Texas, which are already the most congested corners of the network. It is the equivalent of a whole new city deciding to move into one neighbourhood.

"The consequences are already visible. Transmission congestion cost an estimated $11 billion in 2023.Grids in Transition: US · The Next 25

What a jam looks like on a grid

When the roads are full, you pay in two ways. First, in money: moving power around the bottlenecks cost the system an estimated $11 billion in 2023 alone. Second, in waste: in California and Texas, wind and solar farms are being told to switch off because there is no line to carry what they produce.

And sometimes you pay in a fright. In July 2026 a routine fault on a transmission line in Northern Virginia pushed around 3.8 gigawatts of data-centre load onto backup generators in an instant, and the voltage and frequency of the surrounding grid wobbled. Nothing broke. But it showed what happens when a great deal of critical demand sits in one place.

Rebuilding ahead of the traffic

The report's answer is not one thing but four at once: faster and smarter planning and permitting, technology that gets more out of existing lines and keeps the system stable, a supply chain that can actually deliver the equipment, and markets that reward building ahead of need rather than after the outage.

That last part is where Hitachi Energy comes in. It has committed a historic $1 billion to American manufacturing, is breaking ground on the country's largest large-power-transformer facility in Virginia, and builds the HVDC links and grid-stabilising equipment that let a region absorb a gigawatt of new demand without the lights flickering. It is unglamorous work. It is also the only kind that fixes a jam.
