Space and the grid are now the same system

Getting to orbit got ninety per cent cheaper, and almost everything you did today depended on it. The relationship runs both ways.

The Next 25TopicThe electricity era · 2026
Scroll to read

For fifty years, going to space cost roughly what it had always cost. Then it collapsed. Putting a kilogram into low Earth orbit cost about $54,500 on the Space Shuttle. Today, on a reused rocket, it is somewhere around $1,500. When the price of reaching somewhere falls by ninety per cent, that place stops being a destination and becomes infrastructure.

The sky filled up. More than 10,000 active satellites now belong to a single constellation, roughly two-thirds of everything humanity has operating in orbit. And more than sixty per cent of the space economy's growth to 2035 lands in industries that are not space industries at all: supply chains, food and agriculture, retail, communications.

Here is the part almost nobody knows. A modern grid measures itself across continents using timestamps accurate to one microsecond, taken from satellites, usually with no backup. And space can switch the grid off: in March 1989 a geomagnetic storm collapsed Quebec's entire network in ninety seconds, leaving six million people without power.
