AI & the grid · Feature

Why your AI prompt ends at a substation

The next decade of artificial intelligence will not be decided by code. It will be decided by copper, transformers and the power lines quietly holding the whole thing up.

By The Next 25Feature · 5 min readThe electricity era · 2026
Scroll to read

We are living through a digital gold rush, but the engines of this one don't run on steam. They run on megawatts. Every time you ask a model to draft an email, summarize a contract or generate an image, you reach into a vast, largely invisible network of data centers, drawing power at a scale earlier generations never imagined.

The story of AI is almost always told in software. The real constraint is physical. Behind every answer on your screen is a chain of generation, transmission and transformation that has to hold instantly, and every single time.

The power paradox

AI has hit a cultural tipping point, and with it a hard physical one: compute is scaling faster than the grids that feed it. Hyperscalers are racing to lock in power purchase agreements and build private substations, because the scarce resource is no longer only talent or chips. It is reliable, clean, available electricity.

That's the paradox of the era. To build the most advanced technology in human history, we first have to rebuild the most basic infrastructure underneath it.

"The bottleneck to artificial intelligence isn't the model. It's the megawatt.Gridlocked? · The Next 25

The bottleneck isn't the chip

Ask an engineer why a new data center waits months to come online, and they rarely start with silicon. They start with interconnection: the queue to connect to a grid that was never designed for gigawatt-scale, always-on demand arriving in a single location. Faster chips help efficiency. They don't lay a transmission line.

This is where the AI conversation runs straight into the question at the heart of The Next 25: the world doesn't lack power. It lacks the grid to move it to where, and when, it's needed.

Rebuilding the backbone

The fix rarely makes headlines. It is HVDC links that move clean power across continents with minimal loss. It is grid-orchestration software that balances supply and demand in real time. And it is the discipline to invest ahead of demand, building capacity before the crisis makes it obvious to everyone at once.

It is essential, unglamorous work that Hitachi Energy has been doing for decades, now suddenly on the critical path of the most watched technology on earth. Because the smartest software in the world is still just an expensive paperweight without the power to run it.
